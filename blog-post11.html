I didn’t get the job: challenges, lessons, and my plan

Nov 2026  ·  vlog  ·  career notes

I applied for a job I really wanted, went through the process, and did not get it. This is the post I would have wanted to read afterwards, so I am writing it plainly.

How it felt

It stung more than I expected. I had prepared, I had projects to show, and I still heard no. The first reaction was to doubt everything, including the projects and the degree. That reaction is normal, and it is also not accurate. A rejection tells you about one decision made with limited information, not about your whole ability.

Why it is hard right now

Honest list of challenges as I see them:

Competition. Entry-level and junior roles attract a very large number of applicants, many with similar-looking resumes. A recruiter may spend seconds on each one.

Experience gap. Most postings ask for production experience. Teaching, research, and personal projects are real work, but I have to translate them into the language of shipped systems and outcomes.

AI changed the bar. Generated boilerplate is cheap, so companies look harder for debugging skill, judgment, and ownership.

Interview performance is a separate skill. Knowing the material and explaining it under time pressure are not the same thing.

Too many cold applications. Applying through portals alone has a low response rate.

What I am changing

1. Ask for feedback. A short, polite message to the recruiter asking for one improvement point sometimes gets an answer. Even a no answer is fine.

2. Tailor the resume. Match the top of the resume to the role, lead with the strongest relevant project, and put numbers on results, such as the 52.5% accuracy in the GNN work or the 100+ students supported as a TA.

3. Make projects easier to judge. Clear READMEs, a live demo link, and a short write-up for each project, so a reviewer understands the value in a minute.

4. Practise interviews out loud. Data structures and algorithms, system design, and explaining my own projects, with mock interviews rather than silent study.

5. Use people, not only portals. Referrals, alumni, professors, former supervisors, and LinkedIn conversations. A warm introduction beats a cold application.

6. Widen the target. Internships, co-op, research assistant roles, contract work, and smaller companies are all legitimate ways in while the Master’s is ongoing.

7. Keep a tracker. A simple sheet of applications, dates, follow-ups, and lessons, so the search feels like a process and not a mood.

How I think I get there

There is no trick. It is volume of quality applications, steady skill building, and visible proof of work, repeated until the numbers work. Each rejection is a data point about what to adjust next.

One no is information. It is not a verdict.

If you are in the same place, you are not behind. Keep shipping, keep writing, and keep applying.
